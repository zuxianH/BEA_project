\date{October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
This report develops the Q-system and Wronskian coefficient construction
implemented in the \file{BAE\_Bertini} project. We derive polynomial degrees
from Young-diagram geometry, explain half-unit spectral shifts and monic
QQ normalization, and describe gauge fixing, mixed discrete integration,
and boundary marked points. A standard Young tableau specifies a sequence
of asymptotic seeds; finite-parameter refinement and homotopy continuation
carry each nontrivial growth step to the homogeneous chain. We derive the
Davidenko tangent equation and show a numerical predictor--corrector step,
separating equation residuals, conditioning, and tableau branch identity.
Rigged configurations provide an independent combinatorial count and a
string approximation, while evacuation supplies a numerical root-negation
check. Two explicit examples connect the definitions to calculations:
$(2,1)$ gives an elementary quadratic, and a particular tableau of shape
$(3,2,1)$ gives six Wronskian coefficient equations, an exact cubic, two
coupled nesting levels, and a complex conjugate root pair. All QQ cells
and polynomial Bethe-equation remainders of the six-box solution are
verified, and an accompanying independent calculation reproduces its
box-by-box growth and evacuated partner. TikZ diagrams visualize degrees,
QQ cells, the numerical construction, tableau growth, and the roots.
The existing saved-reference validation and performance results are
reported separately from these pedagogical checks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Physical setting}

Integrable spin chains of rank higher than one admit a \emph{nested}
Bethe ansatz: magnon excitations are organized into several coupled
families of rapidities, one family per node of an auxiliary root system,
and the full spectrum is obtained by solving a coupled system of Bethe
ansatz equations (BAE) across all families simultaneously. For a chain of
length $L$, the spectrum organizes into multiplets labeled by partitions
$\lambda$ of $L$ (Young diagrams); the multiplicity space of a shape $\lambda$ has a basis
labeled by the standard Young tableaux (SYT) of that shape --- fillings of the diagram's $L$ boxes with the integers
$1,\dots,L$ that increase along every row and down every column. This
report is a self-contained account of a computational pipeline that,
given a single SYT, numerically constructs the corresponding Bethe roots.

Rather than attacking the Bethe equations directly in root variables, the
pipeline uses the modern \emph{Q-system} reformulation of the nested Bethe
ansatz, in which the fundamental objects are polynomials $Q_{a,s}(u)$ of
the spectral parameter $u$, attached to nodes $(a,s)$ of a lattice built
from the Young diagram, and required to satisfy a discrete bilinear
functional relation (Section~\ref{sec:qsystem}). The distinguishing
feature of this project is that the Q-system is not solved by treating
the coefficients of every $Q_{a,s}$ as independent unknowns; instead, a
parameterization is constructed as a \textbf{Wronskian
(Casoratian) determinant} of a small number of elementary building
blocks, split into a \emph{bosonic} and a \emph{fermionic} family
according to a chosen \emph{marked point} on the diagram
(Section~\ref{sec:susy}). This is the origin of the name
\emph{(SUSY) Wronskian Bethe Equations} carried by the project.

\subsection{Numerical strategy}

Even after the Wronskian ansatz reduces the problem to finitely many
polynomial equations in finitely many coefficients, those equations must
still be solved numerically for each SYT. The pipeline does this by
introducing a single auxiliary parameter $\Lambda$ --- realized physically
as one chain site's inhomogeneity --- and constructing a combinatorial large-$\Lambda$ approximation associated
with the tableau's growth sequence (Section~\ref{sec:asymptotics}). The physical, homogeneous
point $\Lambda=0$ is then reached by \emph{numerically continuing} this
solution as $\Lambda$ is driven from its large starting value to zero ---
a textbook parameter homotopy continuation problem, which we formalize in
Section~\ref{sec:homotopy}. The tableau itself is built up one box at a
time, each new box's site briefly appearing at large $\Lambda$ and then
being continued into the homogeneous chain, so that a single SYT
computation is in fact a sequence of continuations through its nontrivial subtableaux,
each seeded by the previous one's converged solution
(Section~\ref{sec:sequential}).

The actual path tracking is delegated to \textbf{Bertini}, a numerical
algebraic geometry package implementing adaptive multiple-precision
predictor-corrector continuation, through its Python interface. The
symbolic construction of the Wronskian relations, and the orchestration of
many such continuations across all SYT of a Young diagram, is carried out
in the Wolfram Language; the two layers communicate through an explicit
polynomial system exported to a plain-text file. We describe this
numerical layer formally in Section~\ref{sec:homotopy} and summarize its
software realization in Section~\ref{sec:implementation}.

\subsection{Scope and evidentiary status of this report}

This report distinguishes four kinds of statements: definitions and
algebra derived explicitly here; formulas read directly from the source;
numerical evidence from saved project results; and mathematical results
whose proofs belong to external literature. The equation-number comments
in the Wronskian implementation match the shifted weights, degree
formulas, and Young-diagram Q-function construction of Chernyak,
Leurent, and Volin \cite{chervolin}. The rational Q-system viewpoint and
polynomiality constraints are also explained by Marboe and Volin
\cite{marboevolin}. This report derives the conventions and small examples
rather than claiming to reproduce the full completeness proof.

The ordinary first-column root interpretation is used for the explicit
examples. Auxiliary mixed marked points provide alternative coefficient
parameterizations; they do not by themselves identify a different
physical Hamiltonian. In particular, a low residual checks equations,
whereas the tableau-to-branch assignment requires continuation evidence.
The companion calculation in Section~\ref{sec:worked-321} is an
independent algebraic and numerical check, distinguished from the saved
Wolfram/Bertini runs.

\section{The Q-System Formalism}
\label{sec:qsystem}

\subsection{Conventions and the information carried by a tableau}

A partition $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_\ell)$ of $L$ is a
nonincreasing list of positive row lengths with $\sum_r\lambda_r=L$.
We draw it in English convention: row $r=1$ is at the top, column $c=1$
is at the left. A box has one-based coordinates $(r,c)$; a lattice
vertex has zero-based coordinates $(a,s)$. Confusing these two conventions
produces an off-by-one error in both the degree and asymptotic formulas.
The conjugate partition is $\lambda'_c=\#\{r:\lambda_r\ge c\}$.
A standard Young tableau (SYT) is a filling by $1,\ldots,L$, increasing
strictly along rows and columns. Restricting a tableau $T$ to entries
at most $n$ gives a Young diagram $\lambda^{(n)}$; hence $T$ specifies
\emph{an order of adding boxes}, not just a final shape.

In the ordinary $\mathfrak{gl}_\ell$ interpretation, a shape specifies a
symmetry representation, while different tableaux label solutions in its
multiplicity space. They need not have the same energy. The degeneracy
within a symmetry multiplet and the number of tableaux of one shape are
therefore different notions. In this report the tableau label is attached
to an asymptotic solution branch and carried to the homogeneous point by
the chosen continuation procedure.

All spectral variables are expressed in units in which the elementary
shift is $i$. We write
\[
 f^{[k]}(u)=f\left(u+\frac{ik}{2}\right),\qquad
 f^\pm=f^{[\pm1]},\qquad f^{\pm\pm}=f^{[\pm2]}.
\]
Thus $+$ means $i/2$, not $i$; the double shift is $i$.
A Q-function is a polynomial whose zeros encode a family of rapidities:
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:q-vieta}
 Q(u)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}(u-u_j)
 =u^M+c_1u^{M-1}+\cdots+c_M,\quad
 c_k=(-1)^k\!\sum_{j_1<\cdots<j_k}u_{j_1}\cdots u_{j_k}.
\end{equation}
The coefficients are symmetric in the roots, so permuting roots does not
create a new coefficient solution. A real coefficient vector can encode
complex conjugate roots; reality of coefficients never implies reality
of all Bethe roots. For $M=0$ we set $Q=1$, with an empty root set.

\subsection{Q-functions on the Young diagram: derive the degrees}

At vertex $(a,s)$, delete the first $a$ rows and first $s$ columns. The
number of surviving boxes is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:degree-count}
 M(a,s)=\#\{(r,c)\in\lambda:r>a,\ c>s\}
       =\sum_{r>a}\max(\lambda_r-s,0).
\end{equation}
This counting definition works everywhere. At the vertices used by the
QQ relations, the overlap of the removed rows and columns is a complete
$a\times s$ rectangle, so inclusion--exclusion gives the code's formula
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:degree}
 M(a,s)=L+as-\sum_{r=1}^{a}\lambda_r-\sum_{c=1}^{s}\lambda'_c.
\end{equation}
Do not extrapolate the rectangle formula to arbitrary vertices far outside
the diagram: its overlap assumption can fail there. Boundary vertices
with no surviving boxes have $M=0$ and $Q_{a,s}=1$.
The distinguished vacuum polynomial is fixed data,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:vacuum}
 Q_{0,0}(u)=\prod_{n=1}^{L}(u-\theta_n).
\end{equation}
The $\theta_n$ are site inhomogeneities, not unknown Bethe roots.
For the homogeneous chain, $Q_{0,0}=u^L$.

The hook length of box $(r,c)$ is
$h_{r,c}=\lambda_r-c+\lambda'_c-r+1$: the box itself, the boxes to its
right, and those below it. Removing one more row and column at a QQ cell
removes precisely this hook. Consequently,
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:hook-degree}
 M(a,s)-M(a+1,s+1)&=h_{a+1,s+1},\\
 \label{eq:degree-balance}
 M(a,s)+M(a+1,s+1)-1&=M(a+1,s)+M(a,s+1).
\end{align}
The second equality follows by applying inclusion--exclusion to the
four southeast regions. These identities explain both the degree and
the normalization of the QQ equation.

\subsection{The Hirota (QQ) equation and its normalization}
\label{sec:hirota}

The monic Q-functions must obey, for each box $(a+1,s+1)$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:qq}
 Q_{a,s}^{+}Q_{a+1,s+1}^{-}-Q_{a,s}^{-}Q_{a+1,s+1}^{+}
 =i\bigl(M(a,s)-M(a+1,s+1)\bigr)Q_{a+1,s}Q_{a,s+1}.
\end{equation}
This is a polynomial functional identity in $u$. The underlying rational
Bethe equations are also algebraic after denominators are cleared;
introducing Q-functions changes their organization and polynomiality
constraints, rather than changing a transcendental problem into an
algebraic one.

To see why the prefactor is necessary, let $f=u^p+\cdots$ and
$g=u^q+\cdots$. Since
$(u\pm i/2)^p=u^p\pm ipu^{p-1}/2+\cdots$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:wron-leading}
 f^+g^- -f^-g^+=i(p-q)u^{p+q-1}+\cdots.
\end{equation}
The leading $u^{p+q}$ terms cancel. Equation~\eqref{eq:degree-balance}
fixes the degree of the right side, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:hook-degree}
fixes its leading coefficient. A raw determinant is generally not monic;
dropping the factor $i(p-q)$ changes the coefficient equations.

\subsubsection*{How an identity in $u$ becomes equations in coefficients}
Write
$Q_{a,s}=u^{M(a,s)}+\sum_{k=1}^{M(a,s)}c_{a,s,k}u^{M(a,s)-k}$.
Substitute this ansatz into Eq.~\eqref{eq:qq}, expand every shift by the
binomial theorem, and require each coefficient of the resulting
polynomial in $u$ to vanish. This is an identity for all $u$, not an
equation tested at one arbitrarily chosen spectral value.

For $(2,1)$ at homogeneity, set $Q_{1,0}=u+x$, $Q_{0,1}=u+y$,
$Q_{1,1}=1$. The only nontrivial QQ relation is
\[
 (u+i/2)^3-(u-i/2)^3=3i(u+x)(u+y).
\]
Dividing by $3i$ gives
$u^2-1/12=u^2+(x+y)u+xy$, so
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:small-coefficients}
 x+y=0,\qquad xy=-\frac1{12},\qquad
 x=\pm\frac1{2\sqrt3},\quad y=-x.
\end{equation}
The physical root is $-x$, because $Q_{1,0}=u+x$.
This sign is a useful check when comparing coefficient and root files.

\subsection{Casoratians: determinants, gauge fixing, and polynomiality}

Define the two-function discrete Wronskian by
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:wron2}
 \Wron[f,g]=f^+g^- -f^-g^+.
\end{equation}
For $k$ functions, its centered Casoratian is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:casoratian-k}
 \Wron[f_1,\ldots,f_k](u)
 =\det_{1\le r,j\le k}
 f_r\left(u+\frac{i}{2}(k+1-2j)\right).
\end{equation}
For example, the shifts are $(i/2,-i/2)$ for $k=2$ and $(i,0,-i)$ for
$k=3$. A Casoratian uses shifted values; an ordinary Wronskian uses
derivatives. In the small-shift expansion, subtracting nearly identical
columns explains why the leading degree drops by $k(k-1)/2$.
For monic polynomials of distinct descending degrees $d_1>\cdots>d_k$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:casoratian-degree}
 \deg\Wron=\sum_r d_r-\frac{k(k-1)}2,\qquad
 \operatorname{lc}_u\Wron
 =i^{k(k-1)/2}\prod_{r<t}(d_r-d_t).
\end{equation}
One can derive the coefficient by expanding each shifted polynomial,
then taking the determinant of the first $k$ nonvanishing powers of the
shift; the two Vandermonde factors give the product of degree differences
and the power of $i$. For a nonzero polynomial $P$,
$\operatorname{monic}P=P/\operatorname{lc}_u P$. All normalized
determinants below mean division by this nonzero leading coefficient.

For the purely bosonic marked point $(\ell,0)$, use elementary polynomials
$B_r$ of degrees
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:bosonic-degrees}
 d_r=\lambda_r+\ell-r,\qquad
 Q_{a,0}=\operatorname{monic}\Wron[B_{a+1},\ldots,B_\ell].
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:casoratian-degree} gives
$\deg Q_{a,0}=\sum_{r>a}\lambda_r=M(a,0)$ as required.
Adding a lower-degree $B_t$ to $B_r$ with $t>r$ leaves all these nested
determinants unchanged. Fix this freedom by making $B_r$ monic and
setting its coefficients of powers $u^{d_t}$, $t>r$, to zero. This is a
choice of basis, not an extra Bethe equation. It leaves
$d_r-(\ell-r)=\lambda_r$ free coefficients in $B_r$, hence exactly $L$
in total. Without gauge fixing, equivalent elementary bases introduce
redundant unknowns and may make the numerical Jacobian singular.

Determinant identities, rather than an assumed differential equation,
provide the algebraic mechanism. The Desnanot--Jacobi identity
\[
 \det D\,\det D_{ij}^{ij}
 =\det D_i^i\,\det D_j^j-\det D_i^j\,\det D_j^i
\]
relates complementary minors of one matrix. Here $D_i^j$ deletes row
$i$ and column $j$, and $D_{ij}^{ij}$ deletes both indicated rows and
columns; deleting extreme shift
columns produces neighboring shifted Casoratians. After normalizing
minors, these identities yield QQ relations. The precise graded
construction and its completeness are treated in
\cite{chervolin}; here we use its formulas as implemented and verify
all QQ cells explicitly in the six-box example.

In the index-set notation of the next section, the code recursively uses
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:reduction-bos}
 Q_{A_0\cup\{a,b\}|J}
 &=\frac{\Wron[Q_{A_0\cup\{a\}|J},Q_{A_0\cup\{b\}|J}]}{Q_{A_0|J}},\\
 \label{eq:reduction-ferm}
 Q_{A|J_0\cup\{i,j\}}
 &=\frac{\Wron[Q_{A|J_0\cup\{i\}},Q_{A|J_0\cup\{j\}}]}{Q_{A|J_0}},
\end{align}
with conventions for raw normalizations fixed consistently within that
basis. A quotient of polynomials need not be polynomial: if the
numerator is $N(u)$ and the denominator is $D(u)$, Euclidean division
$N=DS+R$, $\deg R<\deg D$, requires $R\equiv0$. Cancelling a nonzero
remainder, or checking a quotient only away from its poles, loses the
polynomiality condition. This distinction matters particularly when
some shifted Q-functions share zeros \cite{marboevolin}.

For intuition only, two solutions of
$X(u+i)+X(u-i)=V(u)X(u)$ obey
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:baxter-toy}
 X(u+i)+X(u-i)=V(u)X(u),\qquad X\in\{f,g\},
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:wronskian-conservation}
 \Wron[f,g]^+-\Wron[f,g]^-
 =g(f^{++}+f^{--})-f(g^{++}+g^{--})=0.
\end{equation}
This illustrates cancellation under shifts. It does not prove the
nonzero-right-hand-side QQ equation, nor does it specify an additional
linear equation that the implementation solves.

\section{The Supersymmetric Wronskian and the Marked-Point Freedom}
\label{sec:susy}

\subsection{A marked point is a boundary coordinate}

Write $\mathrm{mP}=(m,n)=(\lambda_\ast,\nu_\ast)$ for a lattice vertex on
the southeast boundary of the Young diagram, so $M(m,n)=0$.
The $m$ elementary bosonic labels are $1,\ldots,m$; the $n$ elementary
fermionic labels are $1,\ldots,n$. These are auxiliary index sets.
They do not partition the rows of the diagram: there is no general
requirement $m+n=\ell$. The shape must fit the corresponding fat hook,
$\lambda_{m+1}\le n$, and the marked vertex lies on its boundary.
A monotone path from $(0,0)$ to the marked point specifies an ordering
of the bosonic and fermionic removals, hence a nesting/grading choice.
The endpoint and the path are distinct data.

The code enumerates boundary vertices as
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:boundary-points}
 \mathcal E(\lambda)=\{(0,\lambda_1)\}
 \cup\!\bigcup_{a=1}^{\ell-1}\{(a,s):\lambda_{a+1}\le s\le\lambda_a\}
 \cup\{(\ell,s):0\le s\le\lambda_\ell\}.
\end{equation}
The displayed rectangular determinant below applies for $m\ge n$;
random mixed-point selectors in the current workflow restrict further
to $m>n$. The default selector is the deterministic purely bosonic
point $(\ell,0)$. Thus an enumeration of efficient paths is not a claim
that every production run chooses a globally minimal one.

\begin{example}[Marked points for $(2,1)$]
\label{ex:markedpoints21}
The boundary points are $(0,2),(1,1),(1,2),(2,0),(2,1)$.
The purely bosonic point $(2,0)$ and the balanced point $(1,1)$ both
parameterize this shape, although the current random selectors exclude
the balanced point. The example $(1,1)$ below is still useful for
understanding the mixed construction. The point $(0,2)$ requires a
fermionic counterpart of the determinant formula, which we do not
pretend is covered by a matrix with a negative number of bosonic columns.
\end{example}

\subsection{Shifted weights specify elementary degrees}

For a chosen marked point, the code's \file{lambdaMarked} and
\file{nuMarked} give
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:shifted-weights}
 \widehat\lambda_p=\lambda_p-p-n+m\quad(1\le p\le m),\qquad
 \widehat\nu_q=\lambda'_q-q-m+n\quad(1\le q\le n).
\end{equation}
These are degrees, not row lengths. For bosonic and fermionic sets
$A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}$ and $J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$, respectively,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:mixed-degree}
 M_{A|J}=\sum_{p\in A}\widehat\lambda_p+
 \sum_{q\in J}\widehat\nu_q
 -\frac{|A|(|A|-1)}2-\frac{|J|(|J|-1)}2+|A||J|.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\deg Q_{\{p\}|\emptyset}=\widehat\lambda_p$,
$\deg Q_{\emptyset|\{q\}}=\widehat\nu_q$, and
$\deg Q_{\{p\}|\{q\}}=\widehat\lambda_p+\widehat\nu_q+1$.
Inside the marked rectangle the distinguished lattice polynomial is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:lattice-index-map}
 Q_{a,s}=\operatorname{monic}
 Q_{\{a+1,\ldots,m\}|\{s+1,\ldots,n\}}.
\end{equation}
The complementary index convention explains why the vacuum has all
indices and the marked boundary vertex has none. Vertices beyond this
rectangle are reconstructed by QQ relations and boundary data.

For $(3,2,1)$ at $(3,0)$, the bosonic degrees are $(5,3,1)$.
At $(2,1)$ they are $(3,1)$ and the fermionic degree is $1$.
The latter point has $m+n=3$, whereas the also-admissible point $(3,1)$
has $m+n=4$. These concrete checks rule out interpreting a marked point
as a fixed split of three rows.

\subsection{Discrete integration and the super-Wronskian}

Let $B_p=Q_{\{p\}|\emptyset}$ and $F_q=Q_{\emptyset|\{q\}}$ be monic
polynomials of the degrees just specified. Their elementary mixed block
$R_{pq}=Q_{\{p\}|\{q\}}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:mixed-antiderivative}
 R_{pq}^+-R_{pq}^-=B_pF_q.
\end{equation}
The finite-difference operator lowers degree by one:
$\Delta u^d=(u+i/2)^d-(u-i/2)^d=id\,u^{d-1}+\cdots$.
Hence, with $d=\widehat\lambda_p+\widehat\nu_q+1$, the leading
coefficient of $R_{pq}$ is $1/(id)=-i/d$. Match powers in descending
order to determine its other nonconstant coefficients. Its constant
$\kappa_{pq}$ is free because $\Delta\kappa_{pq}=0$. This is the
discrete counterpart of a constant of integration, and must remain an
unknown when matching the vacuum.

The source uses a real coefficient representation and divides the
finite-difference coefficient list by its leading coefficient before
solving. Thus raw factors of $i$ and stored coefficients must not be
identified blindly. For example $\widetilde R=R/i$ has real leading
coefficient $-1/d$, and
$\Delta\widetilde R=-iB_pF_q$; normalizing its leading coefficient
recovers the same triangular algebra. Below, $R$ denotes the raw
convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mixed-antiderivative}; physical lattice
polynomials are always normalized to monic form.

For $m\ge n$, form
\begin{align}
 \mathcal F_{p,q}(u)&=R_{pq}\left(u+\frac{i}{2}(m-n)\right),
 &&1\le p\le m,\quad1\le q\le n,\\
 \mathcal B_{p,j}(u)&=B_p\left(u+\frac{i}{2}(m-n-2j+1)\right),
 &&1\le p\le m,\quad1\le j\le m-n.
\end{align}
The first block has $n$ columns and the second has $m-n$, so the joined
matrix is square, of size $m$. Define the raw super-Wronskian
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:susywronskian}
 \mathcal W(u)=(-1)^{n(m-n)}\det[\mathcal F\mid\mathcal B](u).
\end{equation}
The actual equations passed to the solver are
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:wronskian-relations}
 \operatorname{monic}\mathcal W(u)=\prod_{j=1}^L(u-\theta_j),
\end{equation}
interpreted coefficient by coefficient. The raw determinant and the
monic vacuum are different objects until this normalization is applied.
At $n=0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:susywronskian} is precisely the ordinary
Casoratian of Eq.~\eqref{eq:casoratian-k}.

\begin{example}[Perform the mixed integration for $(2,1)$]
Let $(m,n)=(1,1)$. Both shifted weights equal $1$, so $B=u+b$, $F=u+f$.
Since $\Delta u=i$, $\Delta u^2=2iu$, and
$\Delta u^3=3iu^2-i/4$, direct integration gives
\[
 R=-i\left[\frac{u^3}{3}+\frac{b+f}{2}u^2+
             \left(bf+\frac1{12}\right)u\right]+\kappa.
\]
The determinant is just $R$, with no residual shift. Divide by its
leading coefficient $-i/3$ to get
\[
 \operatorname{monic}R=u^3+\frac32(b+f)u^2+
                         \left(3bf+\frac14\right)u+3i\kappa.
\]
Matching $u^3$ requires $b+f=0$, $bf=-1/12$, and $\kappa=0$.
Moreover $Q_{1,0}=F=u+f$ and $Q_{0,1}=B=u+b$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lattice-index-map}. This reproduces
Eq.~\eqref{eq:small-coefficients} using three elementary coefficients,
including the integration constant. The mixed notation has not changed
the physical solution.
\end{example}

\subsection{Two coefficient bases and a practical reconstruction rule}
\label{sec:bases}

The generic basis contains $c_{a,s,k}$ for the monic polynomials at lattice
vertices. The Wronskian basis contains the remaining elementary
coefficients, denoted collectively by $\gamma$ (the source's
\file{SUSYc}), after degree and gauge constraints. The latter basis can
have substantially fewer unknowns. Neither $u$ nor $\Lambda$ belongs to
this unknown vector: $u$ is eliminated by coefficient matching and
$\Lambda$ is an external deformation parameter.

For the purely bosonic construction, calculate nested minors
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bosonic-degrees}, normalize them, and read off their
coefficients. This gives $c$ as polynomial expressions in $\gamma$.
For a general marked point, use Eq.~\eqref{eq:lattice-index-map}, the
recursive reductions, and QQ propagation beyond the marked rectangle.
Always check that every division has zero remainder.
The reverse conversion used to prepare numerical starts selects
coefficient equations containing exactly one as-yet-unknown variable
and only previously known quantities otherwise. Substitution then
continues in triangular order. Failure to find such an equation is a
failure of that elimination route; it is not proof that the physical
system has a continuous family of solutions.

A useful minimal diagnostic is: identify the unknowns, count the
independent equations after gauge fixing, check all expected degrees,
and check every polynomial remainder. These operations establish what
system is being solved before numerical precision enters.

\section{The Deformation Parameter and the Sequential Construction}
\label{sec:asymptotics}

\subsection{The chain inhomogeneity as a homotopy parameter}
\label{sec:lambda-def}

Suppose $T^{(n-1)}$ has already been solved at homogeneity. Introduce the
next site by taking $\theta_n=\Lambda$ and $\theta_j=0$ for $j<n$:
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:vacuum-lambda}
 Q_{0,0}^{(n)}(u;\Lambda)=u^{n-1}(u-\Lambda).
\end{equation}
Thus the target is $\Lambda=0$, while a large positive $\Lambda$ gives a
controlled seed associated with the newly added box. The old solution
supplies initial data; the current $n$-site coefficients are allowed to
move during continuation. They are not held fixed throughout the solve.

\subsection{The asymptotic weights, written explicitly}

Let entry $n$ occupy box $(r_n,c_n)$, and evaluate row and column lengths
in the \emph{current} subdiagram $\lambda^{(n)}$. For a vertex northwest
of this box, $a<r_n$ and $s<c_n$, define
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:theta-rep}
 N^{(n)}_{a,s}
 &=\prod_{j=1}^{a}
 \frac{\lambda_j^{(n)}+r_n-c_n-j}
      {\lambda_j^{(n)}+r_n-c_n-j+1}
 \prod_{k=1}^{s}
 \frac{(\lambda^{(n)})'_k+c_n-r_n-k}
      {(\lambda^{(n)})'_k+c_n-r_n-k+1},\\
 q^{\mathrm{lead},(n)}_{a,s}&=N^{(n)}_{a,s}\Lambda.
\end{align}
Empty products equal $1$, so $N^{(n)}_{0,0}=1$, consistent with the new
vacuum root $\Lambda$. Only vertices northwest of the new box gain one
root. The factors are ratios $(h-1)/h$ of current hook lengths in the
new box's column and row. This is the formula implemented in
\file{initThetaRep}, using the current subtableau rather than the final
shape. In particular, using final-shape hooks at an earlier growth step
can select the wrong branch.

For fully separated inhomogeneities, each surviving box contributes
one leading root $N^{(n)}_{a,s}\theta_n$. The sequential scheme instead
retains the already converged finite roots and appends the new large
root. Its initial polynomial is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:sequential-seed}
 Q^{(n),\mathrm{seed}}_{a,s}(u;\Lambda)=
 \begin{cases}
  (u-N^{(n)}_{a,s}\Lambda)Q^{(n-1)}_{a,s}(u;0),
       &a<r_n,\ s<c_n,\\
  Q^{(n-1)}_{a,s}(u;0),&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Newly relevant old boundary polynomials are interpreted as $1$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:sequential-seed} is leading asymptotic initial data,
not an exact finite-$\Lambda$ solution in general. The source refines it
by minimizing QQ residuals at large finite values, converts the result
to Wronskian coefficients, and solves the Wronskian system before
handing a start point to the tracker. Only a seed with exactly vanishing
residual qualifies for the source's small-tableau shortcut that skips
this minimization.

\begin{example}[An exact check of the large-$\Lambda$ distinction]
For $T_A=\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}$, the new box is $(2,1)$ and
$N^{(3)}_{1,0}=2/3$. The seed root is $2\Lambda/3$.
Yet the exact QQ relation at length three gives
\[
 Q_{1,0}Q_{0,1}
 =\frac{\Delta[u^2(u-\Lambda)]}{3i}
 =u^2-\frac{2\Lambda}{3}u-\frac1{12}.
\]
The root on the large positive branch is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:three-site-path}
 r(\Lambda)=\frac{\Lambda+\sqrt{\Lambda^2+3/4}}{3}
 =\frac{2\Lambda}{3}+\frac1{8\Lambda}+O(\Lambda^{-3}).
\end{equation}
For positive real $\Lambda$, choose the positive square root continuously.
At homogeneity $r(0)=1/(2\sqrt3)$. This proves directly that the leading
seed omits a finite correction; it also fixes the tableau-to-sign
assignment for this chosen path. The other branch tends to zero at
large $\Lambda$ and reaches the negative root at $\Lambda=0$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Sequential construction: which data change at each step?}
\label{sec:sequential}

The tableau defines the chain
$\emptyset=\lambda^{(0)}\subset\lambda^{(1)}\subset\cdots\subset\lambda^{(L)}$.
For each nontrivial step:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Build the current diagram's degree data and gauge-fixed Wronskian
       system $F_n(\gamma,\Lambda)=0$.
 \item Calculate Eq.~\eqref{eq:sequential-seed} using the previous
       homogeneous solution and the newly added box.
 \item Refine at a large finite $\Lambda_0$ until the current system has
       an acceptable start residual. Reconstruct Wronskian coefficients.
 \item Track that one solution branch to $\Lambda=0$, allowing all current
       unknown coefficients to change, and validate the endpoint.
 \item Recover generic coefficients and use them as the next step's input.
\end{enumerate}
A tableau whose initial entries all lie in the first row has an initial
trivial segment. The implementation begins at the first nontrivial
subtableau rather than necessarily running $L-1$ nontrivial tracker calls.
A new row can also change the number of nesting levels and the size of
the coefficient vector; this is why consecutive steps rebuild the system.

\subsection{Extraction of Bethe roots and the root-equation convention}
\label{sec:root-extraction}

At the homogeneous endpoint, the ordinary first-column nesting uses
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:bethe-roots}
 Q_{w,0}(u)=0,\qquad w=1,\ldots,\ell-1.
\end{equation}
There are $\ell-1$ nesting levels, with
$M_w=M(w,0)=\sum_{r>w}\lambda_r$ roots at level $w$.
The file format preserves these levels as separate lists.

For comparison with ordinary, untwisted type-$A$ nested BAE, write
$Q_w=Q_{w,0}$, $Q_0=\prod(u-\theta_n)$ and $Q_\ell=1$.
For a simple root $u_j^{(w)}$ with nonvanishing relevant denominators,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:nested-bae}
 \frac{Q_{w-1}^{+}(u_j^{(w)})}{Q_{w-1}^{-}(u_j^{(w)})}
 \frac{Q_{w+1}^{+}(u_j^{(w)})}{Q_{w+1}^{-}(u_j^{(w)})}
 =-\frac{Q_w^{++}(u_j^{(w)})}{Q_w^{--}(u_j^{(w)})}.
\end{equation}
The minus sign compensates for the self-factor
$(u_j-u_j+i)/(u_j-u_j-i)=-1$ inside the ratio on the right.
Expanding the products therefore gives the usual scattering factors
with shifts $i$ at the same level and $i/2$ at neighboring levels.
Only the first level has the chain-driving factor, since $Q_0$ carries
inhomogeneities and $Q_\ell=1$ contributes none.

A useful explanation of this equation is pole cancellation: a Baxter-type
expression
\[
 \mathcal T_w(u)=\frac{
 Q_{w-1}^{+}Q_{w+1}^{+}Q_w^{--}
 +Q_{w-1}^{-}Q_{w+1}^{-}Q_w^{++}}{Q_w}
\]
must have zero numerator at a simple zero of its denominator. Dividing
by its nonzero shifted factors gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested-bae}.
This is a local check on a nesting path; polynomiality of the full
Q-system supplies additional global conditions. At singular roots,
check polynomial identities or a regularized limit instead of dividing
by zero.

For example, the conditioning of a simple zero follows by perturbing
$Q(r)=0$: $\delta r\simeq-\delta Q(r)/Q'(r)$.
A small coefficient error can thus create a larger root error near a
multiple zero. Track coefficients, but validate both coefficients and
roots; their numerical accuracies need not coincide.

\section{Rigged Configurations, the String Hypothesis, and a Flip Symmetry}
\label{sec:rc}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Strings and vacancy numbers}

Independently of the Wronskian construction above, the classical
\emph{string hypothesis} conjectures that solutions of the (ordinary,
non-super) nested Bethe equations organize into \textbf{strings}: for each
node $a$ of the auxiliary root system, a collection of complex roots
sharing a common real center $v$ and equally spaced along the imaginary
direction,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:string}
  u_k \;=\; v - \frac{i}{2}(s-1-2k), \qquad k=0,1,\dots,s-1,
\end{equation}
for a string of length $s$. A configuration of strings is encoded
combinatorially by a \textbf{rigged configuration}: for each node $a$ and
each string length $s$, a nonnegative integer multiplicity
$n^{(a)}_s$ together with, for every one of those $n^{(a)}_s$ strings, an
integer \textbf{rigging} $J\in\{0,1,\dots,P^{(a)}_s\}$ bounded by the
node's \textbf{vacancy number}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:vacancy}
  P^{(a)}_s \;=\; \delta_{a,1}\,L \;-\; \sum_{b} A_{ab}\sum_{s'} \min(s,s')\, n^{(b)}_{s'},
\end{equation}
where $A_{ab}$ is the Cartan matrix of the auxiliary (type-$A_{n-1}$, in
the cases implemented here) root system. Equation~\eqref{eq:vacancy} is
the standard counting-function/vacancy formula of the string hypothesis:
$\delta_{a,1}L$ is the leading contribution of the physical chain to the
first node, and the Cartan-matrix contraction against the ``string-string
scattering kernel'' $\min(s,s')$ subtracts, for every other string present
at neighboring nodes, the number of momentum slots it excludes at node
$a$. A rigging $J$ for a given string is then, in the usual
interpretation, a choice of one of the $P^{(a)}_s+1$ admissible mode
numbers left available after this exclusion --- exactly analogous to
filling single-particle levels subject to a generalized Pauli principle.
The number of admissible riggings for a fixed string-length content is
therefore
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kostka-count}
  \prod_{a,s} \binom{P^{(a)}_s + n^{(a)}_s}{n^{(a)}_s},
\end{equation}
a product of binomial coefficients. Summing over admissible string
contents with the prescribed root numbers recovers the combinatorial
count of standard Young tableaux; the fixed-content factor alone does
not in general equal the full tableau count.

\begin{example}[Vacancy numbers for $\lambda=(2,1)$]
\label{ex:vacancy21}
The shape $(2,1)$ of Example~\ref{ex:shape21} has a single nesting level
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:bethe-roots}), so its auxiliary root system has a single
node $a=1$, with Cartan entry $A_{11}=2$. Consider the string content with
one string of length $s=1$ and nothing else, $n^{(1)}_1=1$: by
\eqref{eq:vacancy},
\[
  P^{(1)}_1 \;=\; \delta_{1,1}\cdot 3 \;-\; A_{11}\cdot\min(1,1)\cdot n^{(1)}_1
  \;=\; 3 - 2\cdot1\cdot1 \;=\; 1,
\]
so the single string's rigging can be $J=0$ or $J=1$: exactly
$\binom{P+n}{n}=\binom{1+1}{1}=2$ admissible riggings, matching the two
standard Young tableaux $T_A,T_B$ of that shape one for one. This is the
smallest nontrivial instance of the general fact
\eqref{eq:kostka-count}: this particular shape has only one admissible string content, so its
fixed-content rigging count already equals its full tableau count.
\end{example}

\subsection{Why the vacancy formula and binomial factor have this form}

Define the truncated string-box count
\[
 E^{(a)}_s=\sum_{s'\ge1}\min(s,s')n^{(a)}_{s'}.
\]
If the strings at level $a$ are drawn as rows of lengths $s'$, this counts
boxes in their first $s$ columns. For the ordinary type-$A$ Cartan matrix,
$A_{ab}=2\delta_{ab}-\delta_{a,b+1}-\delta_{a,b-1}$,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:vacancy} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:vacancy-local}
 P^{(a)}_s=\delta_{a,1}L-2E^{(a)}_s+E^{(a-1)}_s+E^{(a+1)}_s,
 \qquad E^{(0)}_s=E^{(\ell)}_s=0.
\end{equation}
The neighbors contribute with plus signs after contracting the negative
Cartan off-diagonal entries. The configuration must also satisfy the
root-number constraint $\sum_s s\,n^{(a)}_s=M(a,0)$.
For each occupied string length, admissible highest-weight riggings
require $P^{(a)}_s\ge0$.

For $n$ identical length-$s$ strings and vacancy $P$, their labels form a
weakly ordered list $0\le J_1\le\cdots\le J_n\le P$; exchanging equal-length
strings creates no new configuration. The transformation $K_j=J_j+j$
gives a strictly increasing $n$-subset of $\{1,\ldots,P+n\}$.
There are therefore $\binom{P+n}{n}$ possible riggings. Multiplying over
levels and lengths gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:kostka-count}; summing over all
admissible string partitions is essential for recovering a shape's full
count. A single string content does not generally account for all SYT.

\begin{example}[The full rigging count for $(3,2,1)$]
Here $(M_1,M_2)=(3,1)$, so the level-two string partition is necessarily
$(1)$. The level-one possibilities are $(3)$, $(2,1)$, and $(1,1,1)$.
The vacancy and rigging counts are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Level-one partition & Occupied level-one vacancies & $P^{(2)}_1$ & Count\\
\midrule
$(3)$ & $P^{(1)}_3=1$ & $-1$ & inadmissible\\
$(2,1)$ & $P^{(1)}_1=3$, $P^{(1)}_2=1$ & $0$ & $4\cdot2\cdot1=8$\\
$(1,1,1)$ & $P^{(1)}_1=1$ & $1$ & $\binom{4}{3}\cdot2=8$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For $(2,1)$, for example, $E^{(1)}_1=2$, $E^{(1)}_2=3$,
$E^{(2)}_1=E^{(2)}_2=1$. Thus
$P^{(1)}_1=6-4+1=3$, $P^{(1)}_2=6-6+1=1$, and
$P^{(2)}_1=2-2=0$. For $(3)$ the second-level vacancy is
$1-2=-1$, so it is excluded even though its own level-one vacancy is
positive. Summing $8+8=16$ agrees with the hook-length count.
This counts the shape's combinatorial labels without assuming exact
finite-size roots are perfect strings.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Kerov--Kirillov--Reshetikhin bijection}

The \textbf{Kerov--Kirillov--Reshetikhin (KKR) bijection} is a classical,
constructive correspondence between rigged configurations and standard
Young tableaux (equivalently, crystal paths in a tensor product of
single-box representations). The project implements both directions of
this bijection explicitly, following the classical algorithm: recursively
remove (or add) the box associated with the current largest (or next)
tableau entry, selecting eligible \emph{singular} strings --- those whose riggings
equal their vacancy numbers --- with the length constraints and
tie-breaking rules of the algorithm, and updating vacancy numbers via
\eqref{eq:vacancy} after each such move. The Wronskian solver does not require this string construction.
The separate string solver converts a
rigged configuration into explicit \textbf{mode numbers} for each string
(via a further shift of the rigging by the vacancy number and the string
index) which, through the string ansatz \eqref{eq:string}, give an
explicit approximate (string-hypothesis) prediction for the Bethe roots
associated with a tableau, independent of the exact Wronskian
construction of Sections~\ref{sec:qsystem}--\ref{sec:susy}.

\subsection{A flip involution and a conjectured root symmetry}
\label{sec:flip}

Complementing every string's rigging against its own vacancy number,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:flip}
  J \;\longmapsto\; P^{(a)}_s - J,
\end{equation}
applied simultaneously to every string of a rigged configuration, defines
an involution on rigged configurations (and, via the KKR bijection, an
involution on standard Young tableaux of a fixed shape), which we denote
$\mathrm{SYT}\mapsto\mathrm{Flip}(\mathrm{SYT})$.

\begin{conjecture}[Root negation under the flip]
\label{conj:flip}
For a standard Young tableau $\mathrm{SYT}$ with Bethe roots
$\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}$ (at any fixed nesting level), the flipped tableau's
Bethe roots satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \{u_1,\dots,u_r\} \;=\; \{-u_1,\dots,-u_r\}
  \quad\text{as computed for }\mathrm{Flip}(\mathrm{SYT}).
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

This statement is not derived in the codebase from first principles; it
is used purely as an \emph{empirical, numerically checked} consistency
condition (Section~\ref{sec:validation-flip}). Combinatorially, the flip
\eqref{eq:flip} coincides, on standard Young tableaux, with
\textbf{Sch\"utzenberger evacuation} --- the classical involution obtained
by repeatedly removing the box containing the tableau's smallest entry via
jeu-de-taquin slides and relabeling --- a fact from crystal-base theory
(the relevant crystal-path convention identifies the two involutions). The project exploits this coincidence
purely as a fast, dependency-free way to compute the flipped tableau
without constructing its rigged configuration explicitly, and treats
Conjecture~\ref{conj:flip} as an independent numerical cross-check on the
Wronskian-continuation output of Sections~\ref{sec:qsystem}--\ref{sec:asymptotics}
rather than as a derivation of it.

\section{Numerical Continuation: Formalism and Realization}

\subsection{A concrete warm-up example}
\label{sec:warmup}

Before setting up the general formalism, it is worth fixing ideas with the
simplest possible instance, reproduced directly from the project's own
introductory material. Take the target system
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:toy-target}
  F(x,y) \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} x^2+y^2-1 \\ x+y \end{pmatrix} \;=\; 0,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the intersection of the unit circle with the line $y=-x$, with
exact solutions $(x,y)=(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\mp\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$. This
system has no free parameter to continue in, so one instead introduces an
artificial \emph{start system} $G(x,y)=0$ of the same degrees ($2$ and
$1$, respectively) with known solutions --- a \textbf{total-degree start
system} --- and a homotopy $H=(1-t)F+tG$ exactly as in
\eqref{eq:homotopy-general} below. Bézout's theorem bounds the number of
solution paths that must be tracked by the product of the two degrees,
$2\times1=2$; correspondingly there are two start points to track, one
per path, from $t=1$ down to $t=0$. Tracking both paths with an adaptive
multiple-precision corrector (as described below) recovers the two exact
intersection points to the tracker's tolerance, with real parts
$\approx\pm0.70710678\ldots=\pm1/\sqrt2$ --- this is exactly what one
finds running Bertini on \eqref{eq:toy-target}, and it is the same
mechanism, applied to a genuine polynomial system with no natural
deformation parameter of its own, that Section~\ref{sec:parameter-homotopy}
specializes to the project's actual problem, where instead of an
artificial start system one already has a refined solution at
$\Lambda=\Lambda_0$ to continue from.

\subsection{Homotopy continuation and the Davidenko equation}

Let $F:\mathbb{C}^N\to\mathbb{C}^N$ be the target polynomial system (here,
the Wronskian relations \eqref{eq:wronskian-relations} at $\Lambda=0$,
in the coefficients $\gamma$ of Section~\ref{sec:bases}), and let $G$ be a
\emph{start system} with known solution(s), continuously connected to $F$
by a homotopy
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:homotopy-general}
  H(x,t) \;=\; (1-t)\,F(x) + t\,G(x), \qquad t\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
so that $H(x,1)=G(x)$ and $H(x,0)=F(x)$. Assuming the solution path
$x(t)$ defined implicitly by $H(x(t),t)=0$ is smooth, differentiating with
respect to $t$ gives the \textbf{Davidenko differential equation},
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:davidenko}
  J_x H\bigl(x(t),t\bigr)\, \dot x(t) \;+\; \partial_t H\bigl(x(t),t\bigr) \;=\; 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \dot x(t) \;=\; -\bigl[J_x H(x(t),t)\bigr]^{-1}\,\partial_t H\bigl(x(t),t\bigr),
\end{equation}
an ordinary differential equation for the path $x(t)$, provided the
Jacobian $J_x H$ remains nonsingular along the path. Numerically tracking
the path from $t=1$ to $t=0$ then reduces to (i) a \textbf{predictor} step
that numerically integrates \eqref{eq:davidenko} by one of the standard
explicit ODE schemes, and (ii) a \textbf{corrector} step that removes the
resulting drift off the true solution path by Newton iteration,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:newton}
  x_{k+1} \;=\; x_k \;-\; \bigl[J_x H(x_k,t)\bigr]^{-1} H(x_k,t),
\end{equation}
applied at fixed $t$ until convergence to a specified tolerance. This
predictor--corrector scheme, together with \emph{adaptive} control of both
the step size in $t$ and the working numerical precision (raised whenever
the Jacobian in \eqref{eq:newton} becomes ill-conditioned, as happens near
a path singularity), is exactly what Bertini's \textbf{adaptive
multiple-precision tracker} (``AMPTracker'') implements; the several
named predictors available (constant, Euler, Heun, and a family of
embedded Runge--Kutta schemes of increasing order) are simply different
choices of explicit ODE integrator for \eqref{eq:davidenko}, trading
predictor accuracy (and hence achievable step size) against per-step
cost.

\subsection{Specialization: a linear parameter homotopy in
\texorpdfstring{$\Lambda$}{Lambda}}
\label{sec:parameter-homotopy}

The construction of Section~\ref{sec:asymptotics} is a particularly
favorable instance of \eqref{eq:homotopy-general}: rather than requiring
an artificial start system, the Wronskian relations
\eqref{eq:wronskian-relations}, left symbolic in $\Lambda$, already
constitute a one-parameter polynomial family $F_\Lambda(\gamma)=0$ whose
member at a large value $\Lambda=\Lambda_0$ is solved by refining the
combinatorial seed of Section~\ref{sec:asymptotics}, and whose member at $\Lambda=0$
is the physical system. Introducing the affine reparametrization
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:param-path}
  \Lambda(t) \;=\; (1-t)\cdot 0 + t\cdot\Lambda_0 \;=\; t\,\Lambda_0, \qquad t\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
gives $H(\gamma,t):=F_{\Lambda(t)}(\gamma)$, with $H(\gamma,1)=F_{\Lambda_0}(\gamma)$
solved numerically to the start tolerance and $H(\gamma,0)=F_0(\gamma)$ the target; exactly one path
is tracked (there is no Bézout-number multiplicity of start points to
enumerate, since one refined start point has been selected), from $t=1$ to
$t=0$. This is the parameter homotopy actually solved by the numerical
back end: the polynomial system exported at each step of the sequential
construction (Section~\ref{sec:sequential}) is $F_\Lambda(\gamma)$ with
$\Lambda$ left as the single homotopy variable and \eqref{eq:param-path}
substituted in symbolically before the system is handed to the tracker,
together with the refined starting value $\gamma(\Lambda_0)$ from
Section~\ref{sec:asymptotics} as the $t=1$ start point.

\subsection{A predictor--corrector step, with actual numbers}
\label{sec:predictor-detail}

There are three different derivatives to keep separate. The Jacobian
$J_{ij}=\partial F_i/\partial\gamma_j$ differentiates with respect to
unknown coefficients, $F_\Lambda$ differentiates with respect to the
external parameter, and the total derivative along a solution obeys
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:param-davidenko}
 J\frac{d\gamma}{d\Lambda}=-F_\Lambda.
\end{equation}
For Eq.~\eqref{eq:321-polynomial-system},
$F_\Lambda=(1,0,0,0,0,0)^\mathsf T$.
For the tracker parameter $t$, multiply by $d\Lambda/dt=\Lambda_0$.
In practice solve this linear system for the tangent; do not explicitly
form a matrix inverse.

At $(\gamma_j,\Lambda_j)$, an Euler predictor gives
$\gamma_{\rm pred}=\gamma_j+\delta\Lambda\,v_j$ with
$J_jv_j=-F_\Lambda$. Newton correction at the new, fixed parameter is
\[
 J(\gamma^{(k)},\Lambda_{j+1})\,\delta\gamma^{(k)}
 =-F(\gamma^{(k)},\Lambda_{j+1}),\qquad
 \gamma^{(k+1)}=\gamma^{(k)}+\delta\gamma^{(k)}.
\]
The predictor estimates a nearby point on the same path; the corrector
solves the new fiber. Newton convergence by itself does not identify
which branch was found.

Use the exact three-site example to see the numbers. Its physical root
obeys $g(r,\Lambda)=r^2-2\Lambda r/3-1/12=0$ and
\[
 \frac{dr}{d\Lambda}=\frac{2r/3}{2r-2\Lambda/3}
 =\frac13\left(1+\frac{\Lambda}{\sqrt{\Lambda^2+3/4}}\right)
\]
on the positive branch. Starting at $\Lambda=1$, the root is
$r=0.774291885177\ldots$. Taking $\delta\Lambda=-0.1$ gives
$r_{\rm pred}=0.715760920310\ldots$.
One Newton update at $\Lambda=0.9$ is
\[
 r_{\rm new}=r_{\rm pred}
 -\frac{r_{\rm pred}^2-0.6r_{\rm pred}-1/12}
             {2r_{\rm pred}-0.6}
 =0.716333593754\ldots,
\]
while the exact new root is $0.716333199893\ldots$.
Further corrections remove the remaining error. The only approximation
in this illustration is the finite predictor step and its stopped
Newton correction; the polynomial family is exact.

\subsection{Residuals, conditioning, and branch identity}

A residual $\|F(\widehat\gamma,\Lambda)\|$ measures equation error.
For a nearby regular solution, its linearized coefficient error is
$\delta\gamma\simeq-J^{-1}F$. Hence a tiny residual can coexist with a
larger coefficient error if $J$ is ill-conditioned. Rescaling individual
equations changes the numerical residual norm, so compare residuals in
a stated normalization; it does not change the zero set.
A practical endpoint check combines the following independent questions:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Are all coefficients finite, and is the exported real
       representation consistent within the requested tolerance?
 \item Does the monic vacuum determinant match $u^L$ coefficient by
       coefficient, and do reconstructed QQ relations have small residuals?
 \item Do polynomial divisions have vanishing remainders and the prescribed
       degrees, including boundary values?
 \item Are the roots grouped correctly by nesting level, and do the
       nonsingular nested BAE or their polynomial forms hold?
 \item Does continuation retain the intended tableau branch, as checked
       by reproducibility, reverse tracking, or comparison with a flip partner?
\end{enumerate}
The final question is different from the first four: a different branch
may solve the same endpoint equations perfectly. A flip check is useful
additional evidence but cannot alone certify every tableau label.

\subsection{Numerical safeguards}

Two safeguards are imposed around the tracked endpoint before it is
accepted as a physical solution. First, because the current interface exports a real representation of the
tracked polynomial coefficients,
each returned coefficient $\gamma=\gamma_{\mathrm{re}}+i\gamma_{\mathrm{im}}$
is required to satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:real-check}
  |\gamma_{\mathrm{im}}| \;\le\; \varepsilon_{\mathrm{track}}\cdot\max(1,|\gamma_{\mathrm{re}}|),
\end{equation}
for the tracker's own tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{track}}$, with any
violation (or any non-finite value) aborting the run rather than
silently truncating an unexpectedly complex endpoint. Second, every
numerical result is published to disk only by an atomic replace of a
completed temporary file, so that a reader can never observe a partially
written solution, and a failed or timed-out continuation simply leaves no
new output rather than a corrupted one.

\subsection{A path-tracking failure mode: branch jumps}
\label{sec:branch-jump-formal}

The predictor--corrector scheme of \eqref{eq:davidenko}--\eqref{eq:newton}
is only guaranteed to follow the correct solution branch if the step size
in $t$ is small enough, relative to the local curvature of the path and
the separation of nearby solution branches, for the corrector to converge
back to the \emph{same} branch the predictor was following. When this
fails --- typically because the path passes close to another solution
branch or to a singularity of the discriminant locus --- the tracker can
converge to a numerically valid but physically incorrect solution: a
\emph{branch jump}. Empirically, for a pair of tableaux related by the
flip involution of Section~\ref{sec:flip} (whose roots are conjectured to
be exact negatives of one another, Conjecture~\ref{conj:flip}), starting
the continuation from a substantially larger $\Lambda_0$ and tracking at
higher precision and tighter tolerance was found to eliminate an observed
branch-jump discrepancy entirely (Section~\ref{sec:validation-flip}); a larger $\Lambda_0$ can improve the asymptotic seed but increases
the parameter interval to be tracked. Higher precision reduces round-off,
and tighter step control helps keep Newton correction on the intended
branch. Neither change alone is a formal branch-identity guarantee.

\begin{remark}[Why extra precision helps]
It is worth making the mechanism a little more concrete. Two solution
branches that pass close to one another correspond to the Jacobian
$J_xH(x(t),t)$ in \eqref{eq:newton} becoming nearly singular somewhere
along the path (formally, the path approaches the \emph{discriminant
locus} where two roots of the system coincide). Near such a point, the
Newton correction $[J_xH]^{-1}H$ is the product of a huge, ill-conditioned
inverse and a small residual, so the correction's accuracy is extremely
sensitive to round-off in $H$ and $J_xH$ themselves: at fixed (say,
machine) precision, the correction step can easily be thrown to the
\emph{wrong} one of the two nearby branches. Raising the working
precision shrinks the round-off in $H$ and $J_xH$ directly, which is
exactly what lets the corrector resolve which of the two nearby branches
it started closest to --- this is the sense in which \emph{adaptive}
precision (raised locally, only where the path actually passes near such
a point, rather than uniformly everywhere) is doing real, not merely
cosmetic, numerical work.
\end{remark}

\section{A Fully Worked Example: the Shape \texorpdfstring{$(2,1)$}{(2,1)}}
\label{sec:worked-example}

The coefficient calculation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:small-coefficients} already
solves the complete Q-system. It is useful to recover the same roots from
the ordinary one-magnon BAE, for a homogeneous length-three chain:
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:one-magnon}
 \left(\frac{u+i/2}{u-i/2}\right)^L=1.
\end{equation}
At $L=3$, the difference of cubes is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:one-magnon-quadratic}
 (u+i/2)^3-(u-i/2)^3=i(3u^2-1/4)=0,
 \qquad u=\pm\frac1{2\sqrt3}.
\end{equation}
Neither root makes the denominator zero. The two solutions agree with
the roots $-x$ of $Q_{1,0}=u+x$ found from QQ coefficient matching.
Equation~\eqref{eq:three-site-path} fixes $T_A$ to the positive root for
the specified positive-real inhomogeneity path; $T_B$ gives the other
branch. The shape alone determines the pair, while the path determines
the assignment.

Evacuation exchanges $T_A$ and $T_B$. To check it explicitly, delete $1$
from $T_A$, slide $2$ from the right into the hole (it is smaller than
the lower neighbor $3$), and record $3$ at exit box $(1,2)$. Decrement
the remaining entries. The next deletion exits at $(2,1)$, where one
records $2$; the last exits at $(1,1)$, where one records $1$. The result
is $T_B$. Their root sets are exact negatives, so the level-wise flip
comparison vanishes. This is a complete warm-up with one root; the next
section treats coupled nesting levels and a nonreal root pair.

\subsection{Count degrees and write every unknown}

The positive-degree polynomials are $Q_{0,0}$ of degree six,
$Q_{1,0},Q_{0,1}$ of degree three, and $Q_{2,0},Q_{1,1},Q_{0,2}$ of
degree one. The required zero-degree boundary functions are
$Q_{3,0}=Q_{3,1}=Q_{2,1}=Q_{2,2}=Q_{1,2}=Q_{1,3}=Q_{0,3}=1$.
The physical nesting levels contain $M_1=3$ and $M_2=1$ roots.

Use the default purely bosonic marked point $(3,0)$. From
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bosonic-degrees}, take gauge-fixed elementary polynomials
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-elementary}
 B_1=u^5+a u^4+b u^2+c,\qquad
 B_2=u^3+d u^2+e,\qquad B_3=u+f.
\end{equation}
The omitted $u^3,u$ terms of $B_1$, and $u$ term of $B_2$, are gauge
zeros. There are six unknowns $(a,b,c,d,e,f)$.
The normalized vacuum determinant is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-determinant}
 Q_{0,0}^{\mathrm W}(u)=\frac1{-16i}
 \det\begin{pmatrix}
 B_1(u+i)&B_1(u)&B_1(u-i)\\
 B_2(u+i)&B_2(u)&B_2(u-i)\\
 B_3(u+i)&B_3(u)&B_3(u-i)
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The leading coefficient is
$i^3(5-3)(5-1)(3-1)=-16i$, explaining the denominator.
Expand as $u^6+w_5u^5+\cdots+w_0$. Every coefficient is
\begin{align}
 w_5={}&\frac38a+\frac34d+\frac{15}{8}f,\notag\\
 w_4={}&\frac38ad+\frac34af+\frac{15}{8}df+\frac54,\notag\\
 w_3={}&adf+\frac12a-\frac18b+\frac58d-\frac54e+\frac58f,\notag\\
 w_2={}&\frac38ad-\frac34ae+\frac38af-\frac38bf+\frac14,\notag\\
 w_1={}&\frac14adf+\frac18a-\frac18b+\frac38c-\frac18d+
             \frac58e+\frac14f,\notag\\
 w_0={}&\frac18(ae+af-be-bf+cd-df).
 \label{eq:321-wcoeff}
\end{align}
At the final growth step, this is the square parameter system
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-polynomial-system}
 F_6(a,b,c,d,e,f;\Lambda)
 =(w_5+\Lambda,w_4,w_3,w_2,w_1,w_0)=0.

Its homogeneous endpoint is $w_k=0$ for every $k$. No root variable is
tracked in this system; roots are extracted afterward.

 =u^3+\frac{d+3f}{2}u^2+\left(df+\frac14\right)u
       +\frac d8-\frac e2-\frac f8.
 \label{eq:321-basis-conversion}

This is the two-basis conversion worked out explicitly, including its

\subsection{Solve the selected homogeneous branch exactly}

The branch above ends at $f=0$, so $Q_{2,0}=u$.
Do not impose $f=0$ when solving all sixteen tableau branches: here it
is a property of this selected endpoint. With $f=0$, the six equations
Eq.~\eqref{eq:321-polynomial-system} become easy to eliminate:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item $w_5=0$ gives $a=-2d$.
 \item $w_4=0$ gives $3ad/8+5/4=0$, hence $d^2=5/3$.
 \item $w_2=0$ gives $3ad/8-3ae/4+1/4=0$, hence $ae=-4/3$
       and $e=2/(3d)=2d/5$.
 \item $w_3=0$ gives $b=4a+5d-10e=-7d$.
 \item $w_1=0$ gives $c=(-a+b+d-5e)/3=-2d$.
 \item $w_0=0$ is then satisfied: $(a-b)e+cd=5de-2d^2=0$.
\end{enumerate}
The continued solution has $d>0$, so $d=\sqrt{15}/3$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-exact-elementary}
 (a,b,c,d,e,f)=\left(-\frac{2\sqrt{15}}3,-\frac{7\sqrt{15}}3,
 -\frac{2\sqrt{15}}3,\frac{\sqrt{15}}3,\frac{2\sqrt{15}}{15},0\right).
\end{equation}

physical polynomials
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-exact-physical}
 \boxed{Q_{1,0}=u^3+\frac{\sqrt{15}}6u^2+\frac14u-
                     \frac{\sqrt{15}}{40},\qquad Q_{2,0}=u.}
\end{equation}
Both coefficient vectors are real, while $Q_{1,0}$ has two nonreal
zeros. The positive-sign choice was fixed by the branch calculation,

\subsection{Reconstruct the whole Q-system and check every cell}

 =u^2-\frac{2A}{15}u-\frac29
 =(u+2A/3)(u-4A/5),
\]
so $Q_{0,2}=u-4A/5$.
We have now reconstructed every positive-degree polynomial:
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-all-q}
 \begin{array}{ll}
 Q_{0,0}=u^6,& Q_{1,0}=u^3+Au^2+u/4-3A/20,\\
 Q_{0,1}=u^3-Au^2/5-5u/12-A/20,& Q_{2,0}=u,\\
 Q_{1,1}=u+2A/3,&Q_{0,2}=u-4A/5.
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
The remaining QQ cells $(2,0),(1,1),(0,2)$ reduce to
$\Delta(u+\text{constant})=i$, with unit boundary factors.
Thus the six box relations are all verified, including the nontrivial
vacuum relation and both polynomial divisions.

One can also reconstruct the quotient by coefficient matching. Set
$Q_{0,1}=u^3+D u^2+E u+F$ and
$Q_{1,0}=u^3+A u^2+u/4+C$. Equating their product to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:321-vacuum-cell} gives
\begin{align*}
 A/5+D&=0,& AD+E+1/2&=0,\\
 AE+2A/3+C+D/4+F&=0,& AF+CD+E/4+9/80&=0,\\
 -A/20+CE+F/4&=0,&CF-1/320&=0.
\end{align*}
Their solution on this branch is
$D=-A/5$, $E=-5/12$, $C=-3A/20$, $F=-A/20$.
This provides a second, entirely explicit route to the same lattice data.

\subsection{Extract the cubic roots, with no unspecified solver step}

To solve the first polynomial in Eq.~\eqref{eq:321-exact-physical},
put $u=z-A/3$. Since $A^2=5/12$, its depressed cubic is
\[
 z^3+\frac19z-\frac{82A}{405}=0.
\]
Its Cardano discriminant is
\[
 \left(\frac{41A}{405}\right)^2+\left(\frac1{27}\right)^3
 =\frac7{1620}>0.
\]
Let $\sqrt[3]{x}_{\mathbb R}$ denote the real cube root, also for negative
$x$. The real Bethe root is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-cardano}
 r=\sqrt[3]{\frac{41\sqrt{15}}{2430}+\frac{\sqrt{35}}{90}}_{\mathbb R}
  +\sqrt[3]{\frac{41\sqrt{15}}{2430}-\frac{\sqrt{35}}{90}}_{\mathbb R}
  -\frac{\sqrt{15}}{18}.
\end{equation}
Dividing by $u-r$ leaves
$u^2+(A+r)u+(1/4+Ar+r^2)$, so the other two roots are
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:321-complex-pair}
 u_\pm=-\frac{A+r}{2}
       \pm\frac{i}{2}\sqrt{1+2Ar+3r^2-A^2}.
\end{equation}
Numerically, the root lists by nesting level are
\begin{align}
 \{u_j^{(1)}\}&=\{0.2199035743065745,
 -0.4327003993372387\pm0.5030656947652822i\},\notag\\
 \{u_j^{(2)}\}&=\{0\}.
 \label{eq:321-root-values}
\end{align}
These decimals are rounded displays of the exact solution, not additional
independent unknowns.

As an independent root-equation check, insert these polynomials into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested-bae}. At level two, $v=0$ and $Q_3=1$;
$Q_1(i/2)=Q_1(-i/2)=-2A/5$, so the left side is $1$.
The right side is $-Q_2(i)/Q_2(-i)=1$ as well. At level one the
pole-cancellation numerator reduces modulo $Q_1$ to zero; the companion
script checks this exactly, so the cubic roots all satisfy the ordinary
BAE wherever the shifted denominators are nonzero.

\subsection{Strings and evacuation: what this example validates}

The complex pair has center $v\approx-0.432700399337$. Its imaginary
separation is approximately $1.006131389531$. An ideal length-two string would have
roots $v\pm i/2$. The observed deviation of each imaginary part is
$0.0030656947652822$. The exact polynomial solution therefore illustrates
both the usefulness and the finite-size imperfection of the string
picture; its rigged configuration is combinatorial data, not an assertion
that its exact roots have spacing precisely $i$.

Evacuation gives
\[
 \operatorname{Flip}(T)=\begin{array}{ccc}1&2&4\\3&6&\\5&&\end{array}.
\]
To reproduce it, delete the smallest remaining entry, slide the smaller
of the right and lower neighbors into each hole, record $6,5,\ldots,1$
at successive exit boxes, and decrement the remaining entries after each
exit. For this $T$, the exit positions in one-based box coordinates are
\[
 (2,2),\ (3,1),\ (1,3),\ (2,1),\ (1,2),\ (1,1).
\]
The negated polynomial solution is
\[
 \widetilde Q_{a,s}(u)=(-1)^{M(a,s)}Q_{a,s}(-u).
\]
Indeed, reflecting $u$ swaps $+$ and $-$, while the degree balance in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:degree-balance} supplies the compensating sign. The vacuum
$u^6$ and unit boundary polynomials remain unchanged. This proves
reflection invariance of the homogeneous equations. Matching the
reflected solution to the evacuated tableau is the extra label statement
checked empirically in the repository. The companion script independently continues the evacuated tableau
and compares its roots with the negatives of Eq.~\eqref{eq:321-root-values}
level by level at tolerance $10^{-12}$. The checked reference CSV contains
$T$ but does not contain this flip partner; the partner check is therefore
an independent numerical calculation, not a saved-reference comparison.

\section{Software Realization}
\label{sec:implementation}
%=============================================================================

This section summarizes, at the level of architecture rather than
implementation detail, how the mathematical and continuation formalism
is realized as a working pipeline; it is included for completeness and to
ground the validation results of Section~\ref{sec:results}, not as a
substitute for the mathematical description above.

\textbf{Symbolic layer (Wolfram Language).} Given a Young diagram, a
symbolic layer constructs the Q-system lattice, chooses a marked point (purely bosonic by default),
builds the super-Wronskian expression \eqref{eq:susywronskian}, and
derives the Wronskian relations \eqref{eq:wronskian-relations} together
with the two-basis translation of Section~\ref{sec:bases}. A second,
independent module implements the asymptotic construction of
Section~\ref{sec:asymptotics} and drives the sequential, box-by-box
computation of Section~\ref{sec:sequential} for a given standard Young
tableau, delegating each individual continuation step to a pluggable
numerical back end.

\textbf{Numerical back end (Bertini, via Python).} At each continuation
step, the current Wronskian relations (with $\Lambda$ left symbolic per
\eqref{eq:param-path}) and a high-precision starting point are exported to
a plain-text table (one polynomial equation per row, each row carrying
the variable name, its starting value, and its defining expression). A
Python driver parses this table into a genuine polynomial system, forms
the parameter path of \eqref{eq:param-path}, and tracks a single path with
Bertini's adaptive multiple-precision tracker (Section~\ref{sec:homotopy}),
exposing the tracker's tolerance, precision, step-size, Newton-iteration,
and predictor-scheme parameters as configurable settings. The converged
coefficients are validated against the reality condition
\eqref{eq:real-check} and published atomically; the symbolic layer imports
them and proceeds to the next site. \emph{(An earlier version of this
back end delegated the same step to a Julia-based homotopy-continuation
package; several internal option and file names still reflect that
earlier design, though the currently used back end is Bertini only.)}

\textbf{Orchestration and batch execution.} A thin orchestration layer
isolates every numerical global used by the symbolic/asymptotic
construction on a per-task basis, allocates a dedicated working directory
and log files per tableau, and records detailed per-stage timing. A
shell-level batch launcher runs many tableaux (optionally all standard
Young tableaux of a given shape) as independent, parallel operating-system
processes, consolidating their individual results into a single aggregate
table under a file lock, atomically, so that a batch's partial results
remain safely readable while it is still running; a native desktop
application provides the same set of operations (single-tableau runs,
batch runs, the flip cross-check of Section~\ref{sec:validation-flip}, and
result visualization) through a graphical interface, without introducing
any code path distinct from the command-line tools.

\textbf{Visualization.} Bethe roots are visualized either in a notebook
(for interactive inspection and for the negated-overlay comparison used in
the flip check) or by a small, dependency-free tool that renders each
nesting level of \eqref{eq:bethe-roots} as a panel of a hand-written
vector-graphics document, automatically switching to a density-aggregated
rendering when the number of roots in a batch result is too large to plot
individually.

\begin{remark}[Why split the pipeline this way]
The division between a symbolic layer and a numerical back end mirrors the
division between exact symbolic system construction and numerical solution.
Combinatorial seed construction is exact arithmetic, but finite-start
refinement already involves numerical approximation; Bertini then
performs the remaining path tracking. Consequently, the only interface the two layers
need is the plain-text polynomial table described above: the symbolic
layer never needs to know \emph{how} a step's solution was obtained, only
that it converged, which is precisely what allowed the back end to be
replaced (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}'s remark on the earlier Julia
back end) without touching the mathematics of
Sections~\ref{sec:qsystem}--\ref{sec:asymptotics} at all.
\end{remark}

\section{Validation and Numerical Results}
\label{sec:results}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Regression testing}

The numeric-string handling between Bertini's long-decimal output and the
symbolic layer's high-precision numbers is directly tested: a
100-digit decimal value is required to round-trip through export and
import with every digit intact, standard and scientific notations are
required to parse correctly, and malformed or non-finite input is
required to be rejected rather than silently coerced. Separately, a suite
of saved reference tableaux is rerun from scratch and compared against
their saved solutions \emph{level by level} (per Eq.~\eqref{eq:bethe-roots},
never flattened across nesting levels), using an exhaustive one-to-one
matching over root permutations at a default tolerance of $10^{-8}$.

\subsection{Validation of Conjecture~\ref{conj:flip}: the flip check}
\label{sec:validation-flip}

The empirical symmetry of Conjecture~\ref{conj:flip} is checked
numerically by computing, for a tableau and its flip, the
\textbf{symmetric Hausdorff distance} between one root set and the
negation of the other,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:hausdorff}
  d_H(A,B) \;=\; \max\Bigl\{\, \max_{a\in A}\min_{b\in B}|a-b| ,\;\; \max_{b\in B}\min_{a\in A}|a-b| \,\Bigr\},
  \qquad B=-\operatorname{Roots}[\mathrm{Flip}(\mathrm{SYT})],
\end{equation}
computed at high (100-digit) arithmetic precision, with a default pass
tolerance of $10^{-5}$; the check is applied automatically, at the level
of a whole aggregate batch of tableaux, and any pair exceeding tolerance
can be flagged and removed as a likely branch-jump artifact
(Section~\ref{sec:branch-jump-formal}). This is exactly the automated,
large-scale counterpart of the by-hand check carried out in
Section~\ref{sec:worked-example}: there, $d_H$ applied to
$\{+\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt3}\}$ and $-\{-\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt3}\}$ vanishes
identically, since the two single-element sets are literally equal;
for shapes too large to solve in closed form, the same comparison is
instead carried out numerically, at whatever precision the continuation
was run at, on every flip-related pair in a batch.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Summary}

The practical unknowns are gauge-fixed elementary polynomial
coefficients. Young-diagram geometry supplies their degrees and the
monic QQ prefactors. Casoratian determinants and mixed discrete
integration supply the Wronskian equations. A tableau specifies the
sequence of large-root seeds; finite-start refinement and parameter
continuation solve each nontrivial growth step. Reconstructed lattice
polynomials yield roots separately at each nesting level.

The two worked examples make the distinction between shape equations
and tableau branch labels concrete. For $(3,2,1)$, six elementary
coefficients determine all six positive-degree Q-functions; one
selected branch has an exact cubic and a second-level root at zero.
All QQ cells, the nested root equations, the sixteen-label combinatorial
count, and comparison with saved roots can be checked independently.
The illustrative numerical growth calculation and exact algebra checks
are reproducible with the companion script.

\subsection{Limitations}

This report uses the ordinary first-column physical interpretation;
it does not infer a particular supersymmetric Hamiltonian solely from
an auxiliary marked-point choice.
The flip symmetry of Conjecture~\ref{conj:flip} is used, and validated
numerically, but is not derived here from the representation theory of
the underlying algebra. The branch-jump failure mode of
Section~\ref{sec:branch-jump-formal} is mitigated by more conservative
continuation settings but is not eliminated by any formal guarantee, and
should continue to be screened for via the flip check on any newly
computed batch of tableaux. Performance characterization to date covers
only small tableaux; whether fixed overhead continues to dominate genuine
path-tracking cost at large Young-diagram size remains open.

\subsection{Outlook}

Two directions follow directly from the results above. First, since the
dominant cost observed in Table~\ref{tab:profile} is fixed per-call
overhead rather than path-tracking time, reusing a single initialized
symbolic-layer worker across many tableaux (rather than paying kernel
startup once per tableau) is a natural, low-risk optimization for batch
computations. Second, extending the performance profile of
Section~\ref{sec:results} to substantially larger Young diagrams would
determine whether the numerical continuation itself, rather than fixed
overhead, becomes the dominant cost once the Q-system's polynomial
degrees grow, which would in turn indicate where further numerical
effort (tighter path-tracking control, higher base precision, or an
adaptive step-size schedule tuned specifically to this problem's
observed branch-jump risk) is best directed.
